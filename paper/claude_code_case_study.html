Methodology paper · Working with Claude Code

One Coordinate, Three Days.

How an analyst and Claude Code turned a single GPS point into a repeatable satellite-imagery screening workflow, with the exact prompts, the conversations that mattered, and a playbook for doing it again.

Subject: the Yemen earthwork project, 21–23 September 2026 (Tihama coast, Marib, Sept 2026 front)
Prepared for: colleagues and leadership who want to understand or start working this way · Date: 29 September 2026 · Method: review of 12 Claude Code session logs, per Appendix D
Project owner: GitHub bhngyn · Drafted by: Claude (Sonnet 5.5) from the logs, at the owner's request

The headline, in a paragraph

Between the evening of 21 September and the night of 23 September 2026, one analyst and Claude Code went from one coordinate on the Red Sea coast of Yemen to a working method for finding, dating and reviewing military earthworks in public satellite imagery. The record is 12 logged sessions, 41 typed prompts (median 17 words) and 989 tool calls, roughly 24 actions per prompt, with no subagents. The work produced two trench complexes on the Tihama coast and two more at Marib, each confirmed and dated on sub-meter imagery, a region-agnostic screening package (trenchkit, just under 1,000 lines), ten offline HTML review documents, twelve KML/KMZ review files for Google Earth, and re-applications to the September 2026 front and to Marib.

The practices that made it work were unglamorous: a concrete anchor in every request, the analyst's hand-drawn labels as the yardstick, "ask me questions" at design forks, reviewable files instead of chat summaries, and memory notes so a new session could resume without re-briefing. The mistakes were just as instructive, and they are cataloged in §7.

This paper documents a method and its record. It does not independently validate the imagery findings; the confidence statements quoted here were Claude's assessments at the time, and attribution of who built what is analytic judgment at moderate to low confidence.

For leadership: six takeaways

  1. Pilot with an anchor, not a specification. The project began with one coordinate and a data request; the scope grew as results arrived.
  2. Invest in ground truth. The scarce input was an analyst's 185 km of hand-drawn labels, not computing power. It is what made the detector measurable.
  3. Require reviewable outputs. Every deliverable opened in the reviewers' own tools and carried a list of what was not verified.
  4. Keep human sign-off on irreversible or outward-facing steps: deleting, publishing, and attributing who built what.
  5. Count setup as part of the work. Data access, environment notes and saved memory were unglamorous and are what let later sessions start fast.
  6. Decide the publication policy for sensitive locations before sharing (§9). Here the owner chose to keep exact coordinates and attribution; the reasoning is in §9.

1. The project in one page

The project applies open-source imagery to the Yemen conflict. The starting question was narrow: local reports said fighters were digging trenches through farmland on the Tihama plain near Zabid, and a coordinate on the coast marked where one of them began. Was that visible from space, and were there others?

Over three days that question grew into a pipeline. Free radar (Sentinel-1) and optical (Sentinel-2) satellite data were used to screen large areas for new straight-line features. Esri's Wayback archive of sub-meter imagery was then used to confirm and date the candidates by eye. The analyst hand-traced 185 km of trenches in Google Earth, and those labels became the test that told Claude which signals worked and which did not. The result was packaged so it could be pointed at a new region.

What the summary hides

The method was not designed in advance. It accreted: each result made the next question obvious. The day-one prompts were requests for OSM canals and a Google Earth file; by the end of the second day the analyst was asking for a detection package that could be applied to "new regions in Yemen". Nothing in the first prompt says "build a screening pipeline." That is the main point of this paper: a small anchor, a fast loop, and an assistant that keeps state.

Readers come from different backgrounds, so the two vocabularies that recur are defined once here. Both are also used in the quoted conversations in §6.

Claude Code terms

Session
One continuous conversation with Claude in a project folder. Each has an ID; this project has 12.

One action Claude takes: run a command (Bash), read a file (Read), search the web, send you a file.

A small text file Claude writes to remember facts across sessions (environment quirks, project status, your preferences).
Skill
A reusable instruction pack, e.g. a house style for reports. Invoked by name.

Long downloads or computations run while Claude keeps working; Monitor wakes it when something happens.

A multiple-choice prompt Claude can show you to settle a design decision.
Fork
A copy of a conversation continued separately.

Imagery terms

Sentinel-2 (S2)
Free optical satellite, 10 m pixels, revisit every few days. Cloud-limited.
Sentinel-1 (S1)
Free radar satellite. Sees through cloud; bright where ground is rough or angled, such as fresh berms.
Esri Wayback
An archive of Esri's world imagery releases, often 0.3–0.6 m. Each image has a capture date that differs from the release date.
Earth Engine (EE)
Google's platform for processing satellite archives in the cloud.
KML / KMZ
File formats Google Earth opens: lines, pins, folders, notes.
Recall at 0.5 % false alarms
Of the true trench length, what share does a map find when only 0.5 % of non-trench ground is flagged.
dB change
Radar brightness difference between two dates, in decibels.

One prompt ("are there similar structures in the marib frontline?") ran a session of about two hours that ended in two dated finds. The final eight prompts, mostly short corrections, turned a folder of imagery into a 420 MB self-contained review page with in-browser downloads.

4. The working loop

Beneath the variety, almost every exchange followed the same six-step loop. The analyst did two of the steps (framing and reviewing); Claude did the other four. Most laps began with a prompt of about 17 words.

Figure 3. The loop as observed. Step 3, checking itself, is where most of the quality came from: Claude opened its own contact sheets, found problems (a color-bar overlap, black mosaics, a mislabeled feature) and said so.
The analyst supplied
Anchors, ground truth, verdicts, taste
Claude supplied
Data plumbing, statistics, review tooling, writing
Neither alone could do
Tune a detector against real labels overnight

Two structural features are worth naming. First, the analyst almost never described how. Prompts said what they wanted to see ("show me on sentinel 2 what you find") and in what form ("create a kml for me to review"). Second, the review artifact was chosen to fit the analyst's own tools: KML for Google Earth, offline HTML with verdict boxes that export to JSON, GeoTIFFs for QGIS. The deliverable was the interface.

5. Thirteen lessons and best practices

Each lesson is anchored in something that happened, with the session or prompt number so it can be checked against Appendix B.

1

Start with an anchor and let requests stack

The first prompt was a query for canals within 50 km of a coordinate. Nothing needed to be specified about the eventual goal. Ten short prompts later the project had a timelapse, an NDVI analysis, a dated earthwork and a candidate map (prompts 1–10). The median prompt across the whole project was 17 words; the longest was 84.

Do this: begin with a location, a file or a single question. Each answer will suggest the next one. Write the long brief only when you have evidence for it (prompt 26).
2

Make Claude look before it builds

In nearly every session the first several actions were reads: memory notes, the project tree, existing scripts. When the analyst supplied a new KMZ, Claude wrote "There's an existing Wayback toolkit in the project. Let me read it so I reuse it rather than rewrite"; the same kit was later reused for the gap fill and the Marib front. For "rerun the latest iteration of the analysis on the original region I requested", Claude searched the earlier sessions' prompts to work out what those phrases meant, and then said how it had read them (Excerpt 10).

Do this: keep vague, reference-heavy prompts ("the original region", "the latest round") but make sure the project has notes Claude can resolve them against.
3

Use "ask me questions" at forks, then actually answer

Two prompts ended with that instruction (21 and 26). The first produced two structured forms with seven questions; the second produced a plain-text list of eight. The answers were short and sometimes were actions: asked about data access, the analyst registered for Earth Engine and asked how to find the key, which answered the question by doing (Excerpt 5). One form answer bounded the work: "write from what is on disk now, defer the other options to be done later after I review what's on disk."

Do this: ask for questions when a choice changes what gets built. Answer in chat, or reply with the next action. Note which questions stayed open; here the design questions sat in a memory note as "answers pending".
4

Ground truth is the currency; make Claude check your labels too

The analyst's early guidance was a single example ("B is the clearest example of known constructed trenches I am interested in"). Claude then discovered the hand trace was 50–100 m off and built a new centerline by snapping it to 0.3 m imagery: "Earlier numbers against the old trace understated the gains." The 185 km KMZ went further: it turned a set of hunches into an evaluation with numbers, and showed that optical red-band change found 1 % of the labeled kilometers while radar change found 34 % (Figure 4).

Do this: hand-label a small, representative example set early, in a format Claude can rasterize (KML/KMZ worked). Ask Claude to validate the labels against the best imagery before trusting scores.
Figure 4. Evidence the labels mattered. Panel A: retuning against Site B raised radar detection of its main trench about 3.5 times at the same false-alarm rate. Panel B: once the analyst's 185 km of labels arrived, the detector could be judged, and optical change turned out to be nearly useless for this feature type. Both panels are Claude's own reported figures from the sessions cited.
5

Let measurements overrule the plan

Three times Claude abandoned its own approach because measurements against known sites or labels said so: an optical-first scan caught only a 1.3 km fragment of Site A, so radar became primary; fusing four maps before extraction lost weak lines, so extraction moved to a per-map union; and a learned ranker (13 clean positives, 24 after widening the definition) did not beat the hand-built score out of sample, so Claude stopped the running chain and made the ranker fall back automatically (Excerpt 6).

Do this: ask for an evaluation harness before optimization, and ask for "the baseline any change must beat" to be stored in a note.
6

Demand a statement of what was not verified

Claude's summaries repeatedly carried sentences such as "I haven't opened any of the layers in a GIS", "I haven't verified the Al-Mashjah rim feature at full resolution, so I'm removing that label from the map rather than claim it", and "couldn't open it in the browser here, so I haven't looked at the rendered page." It also retracted claims: it had named World Bank GIS datasets from memory, then searched and reported that none existed (Excerpt 2), and it corrected a feature it had mislabeled as a wadi channel (Excerpt 1).

Do this: treat unstated verification as unperformed. Ask "what did you not check?" at the end of a big deliverable. Prefer results that include a rejected-examples section.
7

Deliver the file that fits your tools

Claude made 25 file deliveries (SendUserFile calls) during the project. The pattern was consistent: KML for Google Earth (the analyst asked for one three times), contact sheets with the traces drawn thick enough to see, offline HTML with a verdict dropdown and notes per candidate and an "Export verdicts as JSON" button, and GeoTIFFs for QGIS. One report went out as a claude.ai Artifact and the analyst redirected: "i want an offline html not an artifact" (recorded in a feedback note the same day).

Do this: say what the reviewer will open the result in. Ask for a rebuild script beside every report so it can be regenerated when data changes.
8

Put stable facts in memory, not in the next prompt

Ten memory notes were written: environment limits (which Python has the geo stack, 8 GB of RAM, slow downloads), project state (what sites A and B are, the front-line geography used for attribution), toolkit descriptions, the deliverable-format preference, and one note per major result. All seven substantive sessions that started after the first note was written read the notes within their first five actions (six of them as the very first action), and the radar pull for sites A and B started from the site coordinates found there.

Do this: after each major result, ask Claude to save a note with the facts a later session would need and could not recover from the code. Ask it to record deferred tasks explicitly.
9

Turn every repeatable step into a kit, and reuse house styles

Most reports have a builder script, a body template and a figure-prep script beside them. The Wayback fetch script gained --release and --ids arguments in its first session and was reused in later ones. The stack-download script was made idempotent so that rerunning it extends rather than repeats. Two skills supplied house style: analysis-paper (three sessions) and tufte-graphics (once, for the NDVI charts).

Do this: ask for "a script I can rerun" and for parameters at the top. Capture the report style once as a skill.
10

Match the data source to the job, and measure before you commit

Sentinel-1 downloads from one public source ran at 0.1–0.4 MB/s; Claude's own estimate was about a day per orbit for a 100 km region. Earth Engine delivered a 545 × 668-pixel chip in 1.3 seconds, returned all 868 chips without failures, and later fetched Marib composites at roughly three minutes per orbit. Before the big pulls Claude timed a test read (first a public-catalog window, then one Earth Engine chip).

Do this: ask for a throughput test on a small window before any multi-gigabyte pull. Record measured speeds in a note.
11

Run long jobs in the background, and control the noise

The harness blocks foreground sleep chains (four sessions hit that), so Claude used background jobs and the Monitor tool. One monitor was set to report every 500 tile hashes and produced 88 notifications in a single session, most answered with a bare "Waiting." A later monitor that reported only start, finish or error was far quieter.

Do this: tell Claude to monitor for completion or failure, not for progress. Prepare the next stage while a job runs.
12

Short corrections work when the cause gets found

"i only see a black screen" (six words) led Claude to work out that the analyst had opened the unfilled template rather than the built page, rename the template to a non-HTML extension and add a warning message. "I want the download to actually download" led to the reason (Chrome ignores the download attribute for local files it can display) and a fix (build a GeoTIFF inside the page) (Excerpts 8 and 9).

Do this: report the symptom, not a solution. Expect Claude to explain a cause; if it only patches, ask "why did that happen?"
13

Keep irreversible and outward-facing decisions with the human

When moving the 8.4 GB project to an external SSD, Claude verified the copy by checksum and file count and then stopped: "The original ... is still in place. Deleting it is irreversible, so I did not do it on my own." Reports carried confidence tiers, not verdicts. Attribution was labeled geography-based and moderate or low. Claude also declined to treat an unverified social-media claim about Al-Jawf berms as evidence.

Do this: state which actions need your OK (delete, publish, send, spend). Ask for confidence levels and their basis in every assessment.

8. Getting started: a playbook

8.1 Set up once

  • A project folder on a fast disk with room. This one reached 32 GB. Claude moved the first 8.4 GB to an external SSD on day two, verified the copy by checksum and asked before deleting the original.
  • A working Python with the geospatial stack (numpy, OpenCV, scikit-image, rasterio, scipy, matplotlib, scikit-learn). Tell Claude which interpreter; here the system Python 3.9 was the only one.
  • An Earth Engine account and cloud project. Authenticate through the Python library, not the command line. Claude stored the project so scripts needed no arguments. Do not paste the token file or project credentials into chat; this paper's prompt ledger redacts the project ID.
  • Memory on. The first notes written were the environment, a "state of the project" note and the deliverable format.
  • Optional skills for house style: a paper template and a charting guideline.
  • RAM discipline. With 8 GB, two full-frame rasters plus a download already paged; Claude tiled work and ran heavy jobs one at a time.

9. Limits, ethics and confidence

Honest limits of this record

  • Not verified by the author. This paper was drafted by Claude from the session logs. It did not re-run the pipeline or re-view the imagery; findings are as reported in the sessions.
  • Files not opened in their target apps. Claude reported that it validated the KML/KMZ files as XML but had not opened them in Google Earth, and that in-page downloads were tested against a local server but not from file:// in the analyst's own Chrome.
  • Partial checks. The Marib north bank was examined at 0.6 m along only 1.5 km of its ~11 km; the Al-Mashjah rim feature was dropped as unverified; 74 of 113 candidates in the Tihama rerun were flagged "not individually reviewed".
  • Imagery is older than the events. The newest Wayback frames over these sites are from 2025 (Marib: February 2025). Sentinel-2 "after" epochs straddle the 10–11 September offensive, so anything dug in September is hidden in the medians.
  • Sources. Context on the front came largely from outlets aligned with one side citing unnamed local sources. Claude flagged this and treated those items as leads.
  • Attribution rested on geography and timing, not observed units, and was rated moderate at best.

Dual use and responsibility

The same techniques that locate fortifications can be used by any party, and the coordinates in the underlying reports are precise. The analyst chose to keep exact locations and attributions in this record. Readers adapting the method should decide separately what they can responsibly publish, should not treat screening output as proof, and should preserve the rejected-example sections that make a result reviewable. No credentials, tokens or project identifiers are included; the ledger redactions are listed in Appendix D.

What these numbers do and do not say about cost

Token counts are given raw: 1,056,709 output tokens and about 176 million cache-read tokens across all sessions.[3] The cache-read figure counts the same conversation context being re-read on every step, so it measures how long the sessions ran rather than how much text was processed. This paper does not convert usage to a price.

10. Conclusion

In 50 hours, 41 short prompts and 989 tool calls took a single coordinate to dated trench complexes on the Tihama coast and at Marib, a reusable screening package, a fast way to review candidates in the analyst's own tools, and three applications of the method beyond the starting area. The gain came from the loop, not from any single clever prompt: frame, let Claude explore and build, make it check itself, receive a file you can open, review it, and store what was learned.

First qualification. The loop only converges when the human contributes ground truth. The 185 km of hand labels were the single most valuable input; without them Claude would have optimized against its own guesses. Second qualification. The record shows what Claude reported, and Claude reported on itself. The safeguards that made that credible, such as explicit unverified lists, rejected examples, confidence tiers and human approval of irreversible steps, were partly Claude's habit and partly the analyst's choices, and the safest course is to write them down as rules.

The story that doesn't fit in a table

The first request was for irrigation canals. The analyst wanted a map layer for Google Earth, and Claude produced 849 OSM features and a KML. The second request was for a World Bank dataset that did not exist, and Claude said so. By the tenth prompt the analyst was pointing at a coordinate and saying, in effect, there are major trenches here, so learn what they look like. Everything after that followed from a habit that started in the first hour: every answer was a file the analyst could open, question, and correct.
